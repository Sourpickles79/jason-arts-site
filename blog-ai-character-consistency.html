AI Character Consistency: Keep the Same Person Across Shots

A character can look convincing in every individual image and still feel like a different person from shot to shot. The haircut shifts, the coat changes weight, the face ages, or a small piece of equipment appears on the wrong side. Viewers may not name the mistake, but they feel the break in continuity.

AI character consistency starts before motion generation. The most useful preparation is a small set of approved visual decisions that can survive changes in camera angle, lighting, and action. This article offers a practical review process, not a promise that any model will preserve a character perfectly.

Decide which details carry identity

Separate identity from the temporary state of the scene. Facial proportions, age, hair, body build, and recurring costume details usually belong to identity. Wet clothing, a worried expression, or a muddy sleeve may belong to a particular moment.

Write those categories down. A character should be allowed to become tired or frightened without being redesigned. Conversely, a mood change should not be used to excuse a different jawline, a new hairstyle, or an unexplained replacement coat.

The distinction also helps with prompts. Describe the action and expression you need while explicitly preserving the approved face and costume. Avoid adding a fresh paragraph of decorative description every time if it introduces details that were never part of the character.

Build a small reference set that agrees with itself

Start with a clear face view, a useful three-quarter view, and an image showing the costume and proportions. They should all depict the same design at the same stage of the story. More images are not automatically better if half of them are discarded experiments.

Keep originals safe and work with copies selected for the current sequence. Label their purpose in plain language: face, outfit, prop, or location. If a newer image is approved, record that decision rather than assuming the newest file must be authoritative.

For recurring objects, include scale. A bag that doubles in size or a tool that changes length can become a continuity problem even when the person remains recognizable. Companions and animals deserve the same attention as the main character.

Make the next frame belong to the previous one

Before generating a moving shot, compare its proposed opening frame with the preceding shot. Check the direction of travel, the side of the body facing camera, the position of a held object, and the dominant light source.

A close-up should feel like a closer look at the same event. If a sleeve is wet in the wide shot and dry in the insert, the edit will ask the audience to overlook something the production could have caught earlier.

Create a short scene note rather than a new character biography: which hand is occupied, whether the hood is up, where the companion is standing, and what has just happened. Those small states are often where an otherwise convincing sequence falls apart.

Change one demanding variable at a time

For a continuity test, keep the action modest. A head turn or a short walk can reveal identity drift without adding the difficulty of a fast camera move, several characters, and a major lighting change.

If that works, increase the complexity deliberately. Keep the reference set unchanged while testing a new angle. Then test a different action. This makes it easier to understand what failed and avoids spending repeated attempts on a prompt that asks for everything at once.

Kling's reference and keyframe guidance describes those controls as serving different purposes: references guide appearance, while keyframes establish visual states along a sequence. That is a useful distinction, but the frames still need to agree before a model can help connect them.

Review the transition, not just the hero frame

Watch the clip at normal speed, then inspect the moment of the turn, occlusion, or change in expression. A face may return from behind a tree with subtly different proportions. A strap may disappear when an arm moves across it. A clean opening and ending do not guarantee a clean middle.

Make rejection notes specific enough to use: the collar changes shape, the hair becomes longer, or the prop transfers hands. Keep accepted takes labeled separately from experiments. When returning to the project later, you should not need to guess which version was approved.

Sound, editing, and performance also contribute to identity. A visually consistent character can still feel wrong if the rhythm of a reaction or the treatment of a voice changes without a reason.

Leave room for performance

Consistency is not a demand that every image be identical. A character needs to move, react, and exist under different light. The goal is a stable identity inside those changes, not a frozen portrait pasted into new backgrounds.

Review a small sequence as a viewer would experience it. Does this still feel like the same person, in the same world, carrying the same emotional moment forward? That question is more valuable than whether every isolated frame is attractive.
