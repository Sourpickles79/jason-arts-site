What to Include in a Product Render Brief Before Work Starts

A useful product-render brief answers the questions that would otherwise become revisions. What must the customer understand? Which details are fixed? Where will the image appear? A beautiful scene can still be the wrong deliverable if it hides the feature the buyer needs to see.

For furniture and product visualization, preparation is part of making a believable image. The artist needs enough information to make deliberate decisions about geometry, materials, camera, and context. Here is a practical way to organize that information before production begins.

Start with the image's job

An online listing, a lifestyle campaign, and a construction detail are different assignments. The first may need immediate clarity. The second may need a sense of place. The third may need a very specific feature shown without distractions.

State the intended use in a sentence, then identify the audience and the decision the image should support. “Show the cabinet's storage layout clearly for an online product listing” is more actionable than “make a premium image.” It explains what the camera and staging need to prioritize.

Collect any platform requirements at the same time: background treatment, aspect ratio, output dimensions, and whether text or interface elements will overlap the image. Do not assume the final crop can be decided after every composition is approved.

Supply dimensions and identify the authoritative reference

Provide the best available model, drawings, measurements, and photographs. Explain which file is current and which references are only inspiration. If the production model differs from the older product photography, say so before the artist starts matching the wrong version.

Missing dimensions should remain questions rather than quiet guesses. A piece can look plausible while being too deep, too tall, or incorrectly divided. Those differences become especially obvious when it is placed beside familiar objects or shown from multiple angles.

Include hardware, seams, openings, feet, and moving parts where they matter. If a drawer must be shown open, the interior needs enough information to be rendered convincingly too.

Describe materials with evidence

“Walnut” is a starting point, not a complete material specification. Useful references show the intended color, grain scale, direction, sheen, edge treatment, and the way the surface behaves under light. A close-up and an overall product photograph can answer different questions.

Keep approved finishes separate from mood references. A room image might provide a useful lighting direction without providing the right finish for the product. Mark what each reference is meant to establish so an appealing background does not accidentally become a material instruction.

If color accuracy is critical, discuss how it will be reviewed. A screenshot from an unknown display is not the same kind of evidence as an approved physical sample or an agreed production reference.

Agree on cameras before polishing the scene

Make a list of views with a purpose for each one: overall shape, storage, mechanism, hardware, or a detail that differentiates the product. Prioritize the views that answer real customer questions. Several similar angles may add less than one well-chosen detail.

Camera settings affect how a product reads. Autodesk's Physical Camera documentation describes controls for focal length, field of view, and depth of field. Those controls belong to a composition decision; there is no single lens setting that makes every product look correct.

Approve rough framing before spending time on fine materials and decoration. It is easier to move the camera while a scene is simple than to rebuild a finished arrangement around a new crop.

Make lifestyle staging support the product

A room should help establish scale, use, and atmosphere. It should not compete with the object being sold. Identify the styling direction and any restrictions: no people, no visible brands, no objects covering a particular feature, or no accessories that could be mistaken for included components.

Use props with intention. A lamp can explain height; a book can suggest a storage function. Too many accessories can obscure the product's outline and create avoidable questions about what the customer receives.

For a collection, discuss consistency across images. Related products may need a shared camera language and lighting treatment while still receiving compositions suited to their individual shapes.

Define review stages and final files

Agree on what is being approved at each stage. A framing review should focus on cameras and layout. A material review should focus on surfaces and light. If a later change alters approved geometry, recognize that it changes earlier decisions too.

Consolidate feedback where possible. Contradictory comments from several reviewers can turn a straightforward correction into a guessing exercise. A marked-up image with a clear decision is more useful than a vague request to make everything pop.

Finish the brief with a delivery list: number of images, dimensions, formats, backgrounds, naming requirements, and whether alternate crops or animation are included.
